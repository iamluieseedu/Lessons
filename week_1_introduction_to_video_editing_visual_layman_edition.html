Fundamentals of Video Editing

Learning the language of video: how cuts work, why footage is structured like Lego bricks, and the simple rules of storytelling.

What is Video Editing? (The Lego Analogy)

Module 01

Rearranging Clips to Tell a Story

Video editing is simply taking individual recorded video clips, trimming away the boring parts, and stitching them together in a specific order.

Without editing, video is just a pile of random recorded moments. The edit turns chaos into a smooth, exciting story.

The Lego Block Analogy:

Raw video clips are like individual Lego blocks. Video editing is picking the right colored blocks and snapping them together to build a castle!

Raw Video Clips Assembled into a Finished Timeline

Continuity (The Invisible Magic Trick)

Module 01

Making Cuts Feel Seamless

Continuity Editing means making cuts so smooth that the audience doesn't even notice a cut happened.

If an actor raises a cup in a wide shot, they must still be holding the cup when you cut to a close-up shot!

The "Invisible Edit" Rule:

If the audience notices the cut, their brain stops paying attention to the movie. Good editing feels continuous, like watching real life unfold.

Camera Setup & Continuity Alignment Example

Active vs. Passive Viewing Dynamics

Module 01

Waking Up the Viewer's Brain

Passive Viewing

Watching a boring, unedited security camera feed. The audience gets sleepy because nothing guides their attention.

Active Viewing

Fast, energetic edits that show close-ups of reactions. The audience's brain is fully awake, guessing what happens next!

Lesson for Editors:

Your job as an editor is to act like a tour guide for the audience's eyes. Show them exactly what matters at each second!

Engagement Example: Passive vs Active Viewer Reactions

A-Roll (The Main Storyteller)

Module 01

The Backbone of Your Video

A-Roll is the primary video and audio footage that carries the main message or plot.

Think of news anchors speaking, a person being interviewed, or an actor delivering monologue dialogue.

The Singer Analogy:

A-Roll is like the lead singer in a music band. It provides the main lyrics and melody that everything else is built around.

Standard A-Roll Talking Head Interview Visual Setup

B-Roll (The Picture Book / Visual Helper)

Module 01

Extra Footage that Illustrates Words

B-Roll is extra background video played *over* the voice of the speaker to show what they are talking about.

If a chef talks about baking bread (A-Roll), you show video clips of flour mixing and oven baking (B-Roll)!

The Picture Book Metaphor:

A-Roll is someone reading an audiobook to you. B-Roll is opening the picture book so you can see what they are describing!

Overlaying B-Roll Action Footage Over Main Voice Audio

Linear Editing (The Old VHS Tape System)

Module 01

Copying Video in Fixed Order (Tape-to-Tape)

In the past, video editing was Linear. You had to copy scenes from Tape 1 to Tape 2 in strict chronological order (Scene A -> B -> C -> D).

The Biggest Pain: If you made a mistake on Scene A, you had to re-record the ENTIRE tape from start to finish!

Typewriter Analogy:

Linear editing is like using a typewriter. If you make a typo on paragraph 1, you can't just press 'Backspace'—you must re-type the whole page!

Legacy Videotape Tape-to-Tape Sequential Editing System

Non-Linear Editing / NLE (Digital Word Processor)

Module 01

Instant Drag-and-Drop Freedom

Modern video editing software is Non-Linear (NLE). All footage is saved digitally on a computer hard drive.

You can grab any video clip at any time, slice it in half, move it to the front, or delete it without destroying anything else!

Microsoft Word Analogy:

NLE is like typing on Microsoft Word. You can highlight text, copy, paste, delete, and undo mistakes instantly with no hassle!

Modern Non-Linear Digital Editing Workspace

Non-Destructive Editing (The Safety Net)

Module 01

Your Original Files Are Always Safe

When you cut a clip in Premiere Pro, you are NOT cutting the original camera file on your hard drive!

The software only creates "virtual pointers" (instructions) telling the computer when to start playing and when to stop playing.

The Stencil / Trace Paper Analogy:

It's like placing tracing paper over a valuable painting and drawing on it. If you ruin the tracing paper, the original painting remains 100% perfect!

Original File Protection on Hard Drive Storage

Frame Rates (The Flipbook Speed Test)

Module 01

Frames Per Second (FPS) Explained

Video is just a stack of still photographs played really fast. **FPS** stands for how many pictures flash on screen in 1 second.

  • 24 FPS (Cinema Look): Classic movie feel with natural motion blur.
  • 30 FPS (TV & News): Crisp, realistic look used in live TV and news.
  • 60 FPS (Gaming & Slow-Mo): Ultra-smooth motion used for esports and slow-motion video playback.
The Flipbook Test:

Imagine a drawing flipbook. If you flip 24 pages per second, it looks like a movie. If you flip 60 pages per second, the action looks buttery smooth!

Film Strip Frame Density Example (24 vs 30 vs 60 FPS)

Aspect Ratio: 16:9 Horizontal Widescreen

Module 01

The Standard TV & YouTube Shape

Aspect Ratio is the shape rectangle of the video screen (Width vs Height).

16:9 Widescreen (1920x1080): The universal horizontal standard for TV screens, cinema displays, desktop computers, and YouTube.

Human Sight Match:

Our eyes are placed side-by-side on our face, so human eyes naturally see the world in horizontal widescreen format!

Standard 16:9 Horizontal Widescreen Display Frame

Aspect Ratio: 9:16 Vertical Mobile

Module 01

The Smartphone / TikTok Standard

9:16 Vertical (1080x1920): The portrait-oriented video shape made specifically for smartphone screens.

Used for TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts so users don't have to turn their phone sideways.

Editor Warning:

When editing vertical video, keep people's faces right in the middle! Mobile app buttons on TikTok can block text on the top and bottom.

Vertical 9:16 Smartphone Screen Format Example

Walter Murch’s "Rule of Six" (Priority Recipe)

Module 01
Legendary Editor: Walter Murch

How to Judge Every Single Cut

Oscar-winning editor Walter Murch created a 6-rule checklist to know when to cut, ranked from most important to least important:

  • 1. Emotion (51%) — How does the cut feel?
  • 2. Story (23%) — Does it advance the plot?
  • 3. Rhythm (10%) — Does it feel musical?
  • 4. Eye-Trace (7%) — Where are eyes looking?
  • 5. 2D Screen Line (5%) — Camera angle rules.
  • 6. 3D Room Space (4%) — Physical object positions.
Walter Murch's Cut Priority Breakdown

Rule 1 & 2: Emotion (51%) & Story (23%)

Module 01

The Golden 74% of Video Editing

Emotion (51%): Does the cut make the viewer feel sad, happy, or scared at the exact right second? Emotion beats everything!

Story (23%): Does the cut help the audience understand the plot?

The Golden Secret:

If a cut makes the audience cry or laugh at the right moment, nobody will care if a coffee cup on the table moved slightly between shots!

Emotional Acting Priority in Cinema Editing

Rule 3 & 4: Rhythm (10%) & Eye-Trace (7%)

Module 01

The Dance Beat & Focal Pointer

Rhythm (10%): Cutting to the beat of music or natural speaking rhythm so the video flows like a song.

Eye-Trace (7%): Ensuring the audience's eyes don't have to jump across the screen to find the subject in the next shot.

The Laser Pointer Metaphor:

Imagine pointing a red laser at the screen. If Shot 1 puts the laser on the left side, Shot 2 should keep the action on the left side so viewers don't get dizzy!

Visualizing Rhythm and Eye-Trace Motion Flow

Rule 5 & 6: 2D Line (5%) & 3D Space (4%)

Module 01

Keeping Spatial Rules & Physics Logical

2D Screen Line (5%): Respecting the 180-degree camera line so two people talking keep looking at each other.

3D Spatial Continuity (4%): Making sure physical objects stay in the same position in the room from shot to shot.

Why Are These Lowest?

These spatial rules are important, but Walter Murch proved that audiences gladly forgive minor continuity glitches if the Emotion and Story are amazing!

Spatial Camera Placement & Axis Orientation

1895: Edison & Lumières (Zero Cuts Allowed!)

Module 02
Pioneers: Thomas Edison & Lumière Brothers (1895)

The Era of Uncut 50-Second Clips

In 1895, the Lumière brothers projected the world's first movies in Paris. Cameras were set up on a tripod, recorded 50 seconds, and stopped.

Films like *"Arrival of a Train"* had zero cuts. People stared at a single continuous, unedited shot!

Layman Metaphor:

It was like recording a short 50-second Instagram story video on your phone without editing or trimming anything!

Early Cinema 1895 Single-Take Camera Setup

1896: Georges Méliès (The Magic Camera Jam)

Module 02
Pioneer: Georges Méliès (French Magician)

Discovering the "Stop-Trick" Cut by Accident

In 1896, magician Georges Méliès was filming a street in Paris when his camera jammed for a few seconds. He cleared the jam and kept filming.

When he played the movie back, a passing bus instantly vanished and turned into a horse hearse! He realized cutting film creates magic!

The First Special Effect:

Méliès realized you can make people disappear on screen simply by stopping the recording, having them walk away, and recording again!

Georges Méliès Accidental "Stop-Trick" Trick Cut

1903: Edwin S. Porter (Cutting Between Two Places)

Module 02
Pioneer: Edwin S. Porter (1903)

Inventing Cross-Cutting (Parallel Action)

In 1903, Edwin S. Porter directed *"The Great Train Robbery"*. Instead of showing one scene at a time, he cut back and forth between two locations!

He showed robbers tying up a telegraph guy, then cut to police chasing them down horseback!

Why This Changed Everything:

Before Porter, movies only showed one place at a time. Porter proved you can cut between two different places happening at the exact same time!

Edwin S. Porter's Cross-Cutting Parallel Action Example

1918: Lev Kuleshov (The Mind Trick Experiment)

Module 02
Pioneer: Lev Kuleshov (Russian Scientist)

The Kuleshov Effect (Context Changes Meaning)

Kuleshov filmed an actor with a neutral expression. He cut that face next to 3 different images:

  • Face + Bowl of Soup = Audience says: *"He looks hungry!"*
  • Face + Dead Child in Coffin = Audience says: *"He looks sad!"*
  • Face + Pretty Woman = Audience says: *"He looks in love!"*
The Big Discovery:

The actor's face never changed! The audience created emotion inside their own heads based purely on the next picture!

Historical Kuleshov Experiment Visual Context

1925: Sergei Eisenstein (Clash & Collision Editing)

Module 02
Pioneer: Sergei Eisenstein (1925)

Soviet Montage Theory (1 + 1 = 3)

Eisenstein believed cutting shouldn't be peaceful—it should be a collision of two opposing shots to shock the viewer's brain!

In *"Battleship Potemkin"*, he cut between marching soldiers and a baby carriage rolling down steps to create intense panic.

Math Metaphor:

Shot A (Soldier with Gun) + Shot B (Crying Baby) = Shot C in your brain (Fear & Outrage!).

Soviet Intellectual Montage Collision Example

1924: Iwan Serrurier (The Moviola Machine)

Module 02
Inventor: Iwan Serrurier (1924)

The First Movie Preview Machine

Before 1924, editors held film strips up to a magnifying glass over light bulbs to see frames! It was painful and slow.

Serrurier invented the **Moviola**—a machine with a tiny glass screen and foot pedals where editors could view film in motion!

Why It Prevailed:

The Moviola became the standard viewing workbench in Hollywood studios for over 50 years until digital screens arrived!

Upright Moviola Film Viewing & Editing Workbench

Physical Film Splicing (Razor Blades & Glue)

Module 02

Literal Cut & Paste on Film Strips

Old-school editing was a manual trade. Editors sat at tables with literal razor blades, special glue, and tape blocks.

They chopped physical celluloid film strips, glued them together, and hung unused pieces in cloth-lined **Trim Bins** using clothes pins!

Where "Bin" Comes From:

That's why digital folders in Premiere Pro are still called **"Bins"** today! They are named after those physical cloth bins from 100 years ago!

Physical 35mm Celluloid Film Splicing & Trim Bins

1956: Charles Ginsburg (Magnetic Videotape)

Module 02
Pioneer: Charles Ginsburg & Ampex (1956)

Replacing Glass Film with Magnetic Tape

In 1956, Ampex invented the **VR-1000**—the first machine to record video onto magnetic tape instead of developing film in chemical baths.

TV channels could now record live TV shows and broadcast them immediately without waiting days for film processing!

Ampex Magnetic Broadcast Videotape Console

1971: CMX 600 (The $300,000 Computer Controller)

Module 02
Milestone: CMX Systems (1971)

The First Computer-Assisted Tape Editing

The **CMX 600** was a giant computer system using light pens to point at screen monitors and trigger tape decks automatically.

It cost over $300,000 per machine, so only rich TV networks could buy one, but it introduced computer-assisted editing!

Early Computer Controller Terminal Concept

1989: Bill Warner & Avid (Digital Computer Age)

Module 02
Founder: Bill Warner (Avid, 1989)

Digitizing Video onto Macintosh Hard Drives

Bill Warner introduced **Avid Media Composer** on Macintosh computers, turning recorded video into digital files on hard drives.

It created the multi-track timeline, visual timeline tracks, and instant trimming that we still use in software today!

Digital Multi-Track Timeline on Macintosh Workstation

1991: Randy Ubillos & Adobe Premiere 1.0

Module 02
Creator: Randy Ubillos (Adobe, 1991)

Video Editing for Everyday Desktop PCs

In December 1991, Randy Ubillos released **Adobe Premiere 1.0** for Apple Mac computers.

Before Premiere, editing required $100,000 Hollywood equipment. Premiere allowed students and creators to edit video on everyday home PCs!

Desktop PC Video Editing Software Workspace

Modern Era: AI Speech & Cloud Workflows

Module 02

Editing Video by Editing Text Transcripts

Today, NLE software uses Artificial Intelligence to automatically generate speech transcripts of your video clips.

If you delete a word in the text transcript, the software automatically cuts that word out of the video clip instantly!

AI Magic:

Editors no longer spend hours hunting for dialogue. You type a keyword in the search bar, and AI jumps directly to that frame!

NLE Software Interface Anatomy

Demystifying the video editing workspace: understanding the 4 major screen quadrants and essential keyboard tools.

The 4-Quadrant Screen Layout (The 4 Rooms)

Module 03

Every Video Software Uses These 4 Windows

Whether you open Adobe Premiere Pro, Final Cut Pro, or DaVinci Resolve, the screen is always split into 4 functional rooms:

  • Quadrant 1 (Bottom Left): Project Panel (Storage Bin)
  • Quadrant 2 (Top Left): Source Monitor (Inspection Room)
  • Quadrant 3 (Top Right): Program Monitor (Final Movie Screen)
  • Quadrant 4 (Bottom Right): Timeline Canvas (Workbench)
Standard 4-Quadrant NLE Software Screen Architecture

Quadrant 1: Project Panel (The Organized Closet)

Module 03

Where All Your Files Live

The **Project Panel** (Bottom-Left) holds all raw video files, music audio tracks, logos, and photos imported from your computer.

Good editors keep files organized using color-coded digital folders called **Bins** (e.g., `01_FOOTAGE`, `02_AUDIO`, `03_TITLES`).

Toy Box Analogy:

The Project Panel is like a toy box. Before you start playing, you dump all your toys inside so you know where everything is stored!

Organizing Raw Assets Inside Project Bins

Quadrant 2: Source Monitor (The Fitting Room)

Module 03

Testing & Trimming Clips Before Placing Them

Double-clicking a clip in the Project Panel opens it inside the **Source Monitor** (Top-Left).

Press **[I]** on your keyboard to mark an **In-Point** (Start) and **[O]** to mark an **Out-Point** (End) to trim away bad footage before dragging!

The Fitting Room Analogy:

You try on clothes in a fitting room before buying them. The Source Monitor lets you test a clip and trim bad parts before putting it in your main movie!

Setting In/Out Trimming Points in Source Window

Quadrant 3: Program Monitor (The Cinema Screen)

Module 03

Your Movie's Live Output Display

The **Program Monitor** (Top-Right) shows live playback of whatever sits directly under the playhead blue line on your timeline.

It includes overlay guides like **Action Safe (90%)** and **Title Safe (80%)** lines so text isn't cut off on television screens!

Program Output Screen with Title Safe Margins

Quadrant 4: Multi-Track Timeline (The Sandwich Workbench)

Module 03

Arranging Clips Chronologically

The **Timeline Canvas** (Bottom-Right) lays out video clips left-to-right along a time ruler (`00:00:00:00`).

  • Video Tracks (V1, V2, V3): Higher tracks cover up lower tracks (Top layer covers bottom layer).
  • Audio Tracks (A1, A2, A3): All audio tracks play together at the exact same time!
Sandwich Layer Analogy:

Top video track V2 is like cheese placed over meat V1. You only see the top cheese, but you hear both audio layers below!

Multi-Track Video and Audio Stacking Canvas

Default Arrow Pointer

Shortcut [V]: The main pointer tool used to click, select, drag, move, and reposition clips on your timeline workbench.

Whenever you don't know what tool to use, press **[V]** to go back to safety!

Virtual Hand:

Tool 2: Razor Cut Tool [Key C] (Your Virtual Scissors)

Module 03

Slicing Clips in Half

Shortcut [C]: Turns your mouse cursor into a razor blade icon to cut clips into separate pieces at any frame.

Used to slice out unwanted coughs, awkward pauses, or mistakes in recorded interviews.

Pair with Key V:

Press **[C]** to make two cuts around a mistake, then press **[V]** to select the mistake clip and hit `Delete`!

Cutting Timeline Clips into Segments with Razor Tool [C]

Ripple [B] & Rolling [N] (Dynamic Magnet Trimmers)

Module 03

Trimming Without Leaving Empty Gaps

Ripple Edit [B]: Trims a clip and automatically pulls all remaining clips behind it forward so no empty blank gaps are left!

Rolling Edit [N]: Moves the cut border between two clips at the same time without changing the total length of the movie.

Magnet Analogy:

Ripple Edit acts like a magnet. When you delete a clip segment, it snaps the rest of the timeline together instantly!

Ripple Edit [B] Auto-Closing Timeline Black Gaps

Audio Metering (The Traffic Light Loudness Rule)

Module 03

Decibels & Avoiding Ear-Bleeding Distortion

In digital audio, **0 dB is the dangerous roof**. If audio touches 0 dB, the sound clips and turns into harsh static distortion!

  • Voice / Speech Target: Keep voice volume peaking between **-6 dB and -12 dB** (Green to Yellow zone).
  • Background Music Target: Lower background music down to **-18 dB to -24 dB** so speech is crystal clear!
Traffic Light Metering Rule:

Green is good, Yellow is loud and clear, RED is DANGER! Never let your audio meters hit red 0 dB!

Audio Peak Metering Decibel Green-Yellow-Red Zones

Keyframe Animation (Connecting Point A to Point B)

Module 03

Animating Position, Scale, and Opacity

**Keyframes** let you animate movement across time.

You place **Point A** at second 1 (Small size) and **Point B** at second 3 (Large size). The computer automatically calculates the smooth zoom in between!

GPS Destination Analogy:

Keyframes are like setting Start Location A and Destination B on Google Maps. The computer drives the smooth path automatically!

Setting Motion Keyframe Interpolation Points

The 6-Step Editing Recipe (Start to Finish)

Module 03

Systematic Workflow Order

  • 1. Ingest & Organize: Import files into clean Project Bins.
  • 2. Assembly Cut: Lay clips down in rough chronological order.
  • 3. Rough to Fine Cut: Trim bad footage and layer B-Roll illustrations.
  • 4. Audio Mixing: Balance speech volume and background music.
  • 5. Color & Titles: Fix dark footage exposure and add title names.
  • 6. Export & Master: Render final `.MP4` video file!

Codecs vs. Containers (Gift Wrapping vs. Present)

Module 03

Understanding Export Formats

Codec (Compressor): The invisible software engine that shrinks massive video files down (e.g., **H.264**).

Container Format: The file box extension holding video and audio together (e.g., `.MP4`, `.MOV`).

The Gift Box Analogy:

The **Container (.MP4)** is the cardboard gift box. The **Codec (H.264)** is how tightly the sweater inside was folded to fit in the box!

Scenario 1: Fixing Speech Stumbles with B-Roll Band-Aids

Module 04

Problem & Fix

Problem: An interview speaker stumbles over their words: *"I love editing... uh... cough... video."* If you cut out the cough, their face jumps awkwardly on screen (Jump Cut!).

Solution: Cut out the cough on the audio track, slice the video, and place a 3-second B-Roll clip on Track V2 directly over the cut!

B-Roll Band-Aid:

The audio remains smooth, and the viewer sees B-Roll video covering up the ugly cut jump!

Masking Interview Speech Cuts using B-Roll Video Overlays

Scenario 2: Guiding the Eye in Fast Action Scenes

Module 04

Problem & Fix

Problem: In a basketball video, Player 1 throws a pass on the far right side of screen in Shot A, but Shot B cuts to Player 2 catching the ball on the far left side, confusing audience eyes.

Solution: Shift or crop Shot B slightly using motion parameters to align focal points, respecting Murch's Eye-Trace rule!
